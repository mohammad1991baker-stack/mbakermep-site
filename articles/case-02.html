Flush Tank to Flush Valve: A Big Hydraulic Impact

Eng. Mohammad Yacoub Baker · Design notesم. محمد يعقوب بكر · ملاحظات تصميم (المقال باللغة الإنجليزية)

In one of our hospital projects, a seemingly simple architectural decision was made regarding the WC type:

Flush Tank → Flush Valve.

At first glance, it looked like a fixture-selection change.

From the plumbing design side, however, the impact was much bigger.

The project was a hospital of approximately 400 beds, so we decided to check what this change actually meant for the domestic water system.

For a simplified comparison of 400 private WCs, based on the IPC fixture-unit approach:

Flush Tank:
2.2 WSFU/WC → 880 WSFU

Flush Valve:
6.0 WSFU/WC → 2,400 WSFU

That is approximately 173% more fixture-unit loading before diversity is converted into probable flow.

And the impact becomes even clearer at floor level.

For a typical floor with around 40 patient WCs, the calculated probable flow can increase from approximately:

2.5 L/s → 6.2 L/s

when moving from flush tanks to flush valves.

Now consider what this means for a PPR-CT water distribution system.

At a design velocity of around 2 m/s, the theoretical internal diameter required for those two flows increases from approximately:

40 mm ID → 63 mm ID

Depending on the selected PPR-CT series, SDR and actual internal diameter, this can mean moving one or even two commercial pipe sizes up on parts of the distribution network.

And it doesn’t stop at pipe diameter.

Higher flow means:

larger risers and branches, larger valves and fittings, higher friction losses, greater instantaneous demand, and potentially higher booster-pump flow and pressure requirements.

So the cost impact is not simply:

“How much more does the pipe cost?”

It can propagate through the complete system:

Pipe + fittings + valves + supports + insulation + shafts + pump capacity + electrical power + installation.

On large healthcare projects, repeating this across many floors and hundreds of fixtures can turn a seemingly small architectural decision into a significant CAPEX impact.

Interestingly, the sanitary drainage side does not necessarily increase in the same proportion, because drainage sizing is governed primarily by DFUs, fixture characteristics and code requirements, rather than the instantaneous water-supply flow.

This experience was a good reminder:

In hospital design, there is rarely such a thing as “just changing a fixture.”

A decision that looks architectural at room level can become a hydraulic, spatial and commercial decision at building level.

That is why fixture selection should ideally be coordinated between Architecture, MEP and Operations before the domestic water system is finalized.

Reference basis: International Plumbing Code (IPC) – Water Supply Fixture Units and Hunter’s probable-demand methodology.
